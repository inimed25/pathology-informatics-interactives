## Debrief

**Informatics principle:** Unmonitored CDS can become background noise or active harm.

Broad firing criteria, repetitive interruption, uninformative override reasons, and absent outcome measures prevented responsible governance. A universal hard stop would magnify the defect.

The CDS team redesigns and measures the intervention. Clinical stakeholders define appropriate behavior. Governance monitors benefit, burden, unintended consequences, and retirement criteria.



### What This Demonstrates

CDS should be treated as a **sociotechnical system**, meaning its performance depends not only on the underlying logic but also on how clinicians interact with it within their existing workflow. A technically correct recommendation can still fail if it appears at the wrong time, requires additional work, or conflicts with how clinicians make decisions.

An important distinction is between **technical validity and clinical utility**. A rule may accurately identify a patient who meets a predefined criterion while still failing to improve care. Evaluation therefore needs to consider what happens *after* the alert fires, rather than treating successful rule execution as evidence of success.

### Designing for Behavior

The goal of CDS is not simply to deliver information. It is to support a specific clinical decision or behavior. Designing an intervention therefore requires understanding:

* What decision is being made?
* Who is making the decision?
* At what point in the workflow is the decision made?
* What information is available at that moment?
* What action should the clinician take?
* What barriers might prevent that action?

This also highlights why CDS should be developed with the people who actually use it. Clinicians can identify workflow constraints and clinical edge cases that may not be apparent from the underlying data or rule logic.

### Iterative Evaluation

CDS should be evaluated as a continuous improvement process rather than a one-time implementation. Initial deployment can identify unexpected patterns in use, including situations where the logic behaves differently from how designers anticipated.

Changes to the intervention should ideally be made using observed data and user feedback, followed by reassessment. This creates a cycle of **design → implementation → measurement → refinement**.

### A Broader Informatics Question

The central question is ultimately not *“Does the CDS work?”* but **“Does this intervention improve the clinical system in the way we intended?”**

Answering that question requires looking simultaneously at the technology, the clinician, the workflow, the patient, and the resulting outcomes.
