# Faculty Guide

## Teaching Focus

This lesson introduces the clinical microbiology laboratory's role in public-health informatics, with emphasis on electronic laboratory reporting, interoperability, terminology, validation, surveillance data quality, and ongoing governance.

The goal is not to teach learners how to construct HL7 messages. Instead, learners should understand the information pathway well enough to recognize how microbiology data can lose meaning between the laboratory and public-health systems, how changes in the data-generating process can affect surveillance, and how laboratory leadership helps prevent and investigate those failures.

## Key Teaching Points

- Clinical microbiology data can support surveillance, case investigation, outbreak detection, antimicrobial-resistance monitoring, and other public-health activities.
- Electronic laboratory reporting (ELR) supports automated exchange of laboratory information with public-health agencies.
- Electronic case reporting (eCR) is a related but distinct workflow that supports automated exchange of reportable case information from electronic health records.
- **HL7 (Health Level Seven)** provides frameworks for structuring and exchanging healthcare information. Learners should understand its role in information exchange but do not need to construct HL7 messages in this lesson.
- Messaging standards such as HL7 and terminology standards such as LOINC and SNOMED CT address complementary interoperability problems: message structure and transport are not the same as preservation of clinical meaning.
- Successful message transmission or acknowledgment does not establish that the receiving system correctly interpreted every laboratory concept.
- Local test, organism, specimen, and result codes may require controlled mapping to representations understood by receiving systems.
- End-to-end validation should evaluate whether representative information reaches the intended destination and is processed and interpreted as expected, including relevant reporting logic, routing, corrections or amendments, and exceptions.
- Reporting requirements vary by jurisdiction and may change over time.
- Reporting rules, terminology mappings, interfaces, and other workflow components therefore require defined ownership, maintenance, documentation, testing, and change control.
- An apparent surveillance change may reflect a true epidemiologic signal, a change in the data-generating process, or both. Relevant technical factors can include testing practices, mappings, reporting rules, inclusion or deduplication logic, completeness, and interface behavior.
- Genomic similarity can support cluster detection and investigation but should not be taught as proof of direct transmission; genomic findings are interpreted with relevant epidemiologic and contextual information.
- Laboratory leadership contributes microbiology expertise to ensure that electronically exchanged information retains its intended clinical meaning and to help distinguish biological signals from information-system artifacts.

## Common Learner Misconceptions

**“The health department acknowledged the message, so the result must have been interpreted correctly.”**

→ An acknowledgment can demonstrate successful processing at a technical stage without proving that every clinical concept retained its intended meaning.

**“HL7 tells the receiving system what every microbiology concept means.”**

→ Separate message structure and exchange from terminology and semantic mapping. A structurally valid message can still carry an incorrectly mapped concept.

**“Once ELR is validated, the reporting workflow is finished.”**

→ Reporting requirements, mappings, laboratory tests, terminology, interfaces, and receiving-system expectations can change. These workflows require ongoing governance and change control.

**“A change in surveillance counts means the underlying disease frequency changed.”**

→ Consider both epidemiology and the data-generating process. Changes in testing, mappings, reporting rules, completeness, duplication, or interfaces can alter surveillance data.

**“Genomically similar isolates prove direct transmission.”**

→ Genomic relatedness is useful evidence, but public-health interpretation may also require temporal, geographic, exposure, and other epidemiologic context.

## Discussion Prompts

- What information must remain associated with a microbiology result as it moves from the LIS to a public-health system?
- How could an ELR message be technically successful but semantically incorrect?
- What different problems are addressed by HL7 messaging standards and standardized terminologies such as LOINC or SNOMED CT?
- What does a successful interface acknowledgment establish, and what does it not establish?
- How do ELR and eCR differ, and how can their information be complementary?
- What should a laboratory evaluate when a reporting requirement, terminology mapping, or receiving-system specification changes?
- If surveillance counts change after a laboratory modifies testing or terminology, how would you distinguish a possible epidemiologic signal from a data-pipeline effect?
- What additional information would you want before interpreting genomically similar isolates as part of an outbreak or transmission investigation?

## Suggested Teaching Scenario

A public-health agency reports an unexpected increase in cases associated with a particular organism. Interface monitoring shows successful ELR transmission and acknowledgments.

Ask learners what they would investigate before concluding that the increase represents a true epidemiologic change.

Reveal that the laboratory recently:

1. changed its organism dictionary and local-to-standard mappings
2. implemented a new molecular assay
3. updated a jurisdiction-specific reporting rule

Use the scenario to separate three questions:

- **Did the data move?** — technical exchange
- **Did the data retain the intended meaning?** — semantic interoperability
- **Did something change in how the data were generated, selected, or counted?** — surveillance data quality and governance

Then ask what evidence would support a true epidemiologic change versus an information-system artifact.

## Faculty Emphasis

Learners do **not** need to memorize HL7 message segments, build an ELR interface, or memorize jurisdiction-specific lists of reportable diseases.

They **should** leave able to ask:

- What information is being reported, and why?
- How are local laboratory concepts represented for the receiving system?
- Does successful transmission prove correct interpretation?
- Was the workflow validated end to end?
- What happens to corrections, amendments, errors, and exceptions?
- What changed when a previously stable reporting or surveillance pattern changes?
- Could a surveillance signal reflect the data-generating process rather than—or in addition to—biology?
- What contextual information is needed to interpret genomic relatedness?
- Who owns maintenance and change control for the reporting workflow?

## Teaching Principle

> **Follow the information all the way to public health. Ask whether the correct data were selected, represented, transmitted, received, and interpreted with their intended meaning intact—and whether changes in the data pipeline could change the surveillance picture.**
