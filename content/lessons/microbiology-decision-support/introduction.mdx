# AST Informatics & Antimicrobial Decision Support

## Why AST Is an Informatics Problem

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) does more than generate a laboratory measurement.

A susceptibility result may involve:

- an organism identification
- an antimicrobial agent
- an MIC or other susceptibility measurement
- interpretive criteria
- a categorical interpretation
- expert rules or laboratory policies
- comments, suppression rules, or alerts
- downstream reporting to the LIS and EHR

These pieces of information must remain correctly associated as the result moves through laboratory systems.

> **Key idea:** The analytical measurement and the reported interpretation are related, but they are not the same thing.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish an MIC from its categorical susceptibility interpretation.
2. Explain how breakpoints and expected phenotypes contribute to AST interpretation.
3. Distinguish standards-based knowledge, vendor-implemented rules, and locally governed laboratory or stewardship policies.
4. Trace an AST result through expert logic, laboratory information systems, interfaces, reporting rules, and downstream clinical decision support.
5. Explain how configuration changes can affect multiple dependent components of an AST information pathway.
6. Distinguish an individual isolate's AST result from an antibiogram and identify informatics factors that affect cumulative susceptibility data.
7. Explain why AST configurations and decision-support interventions require validation, governance, monitoring, and change control.

## 1. From Measurement to Interpretation

Consider a simplified AST workflow:

    Organism + antimicrobial
              ↓
         AST measurement
              ↓
             MIC
              ↓
      Interpretive criteria
              ↓
    Categorical interpretation
              ↓
        Laboratory report

The **minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)** is an in vitro measurement generated under defined testing conditions.

The MIC itself is not the categorical interpretation.

Interpretive criteria, commonly called **breakpoints**, are applied to susceptibility measurements to determine how the result should be categorized for the relevant organism-antimicrobial combination.

This distinction matters in informatics because the measurement and the interpretation may be stored, processed, or transmitted as separate data elements.

## 2. Breakpoints Are Maintained Knowledge

Imagine that an isolate has an MIC of:

    2 µg/mL

The laboratory later implements updated breakpoint criteria for that organism-antimicrobial combination.

The measured MIC has not changed.

  <div className="micro-visual-copy"><small>Measurement ≠ interpretation</small><strong>Same MIC. Different governed rule.</strong><span>The analytical value can stay fixed while an updated interpretive criterion changes the reported category.</span></div>

However, its categorical interpretation may change because the criteria used to interpret the measurement have changed.

Therefore, an AST information system must know more than the MIC.

It may also need to know:

- the organism
- the antimicrobial
- the testing method
- the applicable interpretive criteria
- the version or configuration of those criteria
- relevant laboratory reporting rules

> **Informatics principle:** A stable analytical value can produce a different interpretation when the knowledge used to interpret it changes.

This makes breakpoint implementation a **configuration, validation, and change-control problem** as well as a microbiology problem.

## 3. Expert Rules

AST systems may apply additional knowledge-based logic to susceptibility data.

These rules may be implemented within an AST instrument, middleware, the LIS, or another component of the laboratory workflow.

Depending on the validated system and laboratory policy, an expert rule may:

- flag an unusual susceptibility pattern
- identify potentially inconsistent results
- suppress selected results
- modify how a result is reported
- prompt additional review or testing

Expert rules can improve consistency, but they do not eliminate the need for microbiology expertise.

Expert rules may also incorporate knowledge about **expected phenotypes** for particular organism-antimicrobial combinations. These are susceptibility patterns that are expected based on established microbiologic knowledge and can help identify results that deserve additional review.

For example, EUCAST identifies *Klebsiella pneumoniae* and ampicillin as an expected resistant phenotype. A susceptibility result that contradicts an expected phenotype should therefore prompt review rather than being accepted automatically.

The term **intrinsic resistance** has historically been used for some of these organism-drug relationships and remains common in microbiology. EUCAST now uses the terminology **expected resistant phenotype** and **expected susceptible phenotype**, emphasizing the expected AST result rather than assuming a single underlying resistance mechanism.

A rule can also create an error if it is:

- outdated
- configured incorrectly
- applied to the wrong organism or antimicrobial
- mapped incorrectly between systems
- changed without appropriate validation

### Where Do AST Rules Come From?

Not all AST logic comes from the same source.

Some logic reflects **published susceptibility standards and microbiologic knowledge**, such as breakpoints and expected phenotypes. Some is implemented through **vendor-configured instrument or software rules**. Other decisions reflect **local laboratory and antimicrobial-stewardship policy**, such as which results are routinely reported, selectively reported, suppressed, or accompanied by comments.

These layers should not be treated as interchangeable.

For example, a local antibiogram can inform antimicrobial stewardship and empiric-treatment strategies, but it does not by itself redefine the breakpoint used to interpret an individual isolate's MIC.

Similarly, an antimicrobial may be tested by the laboratory but intentionally not displayed on the routine clinical report because of a validated selective or cascade-reporting policy.

> **Informatics principle:** A laboratory should know where an AST rule comes from, where it is implemented, who governs it, and how it changes the information ultimately presented to clinicians.

This means that implementing a change may require coordinated updates across more than one component, such as the AST instrument, expert system, middleware, LIS, reporting rules, or downstream clinical decision support.

## 4. Following an AST Result Through the System

A simplified electronic AST pathway might look like:

    AST instrument
          ↓
    Breakpoint / expert logic
          ↓
    Middleware or LIS
          ↓
    Interface
          ↓
    EHR
          ↓
    Clinical team

Suppose the instrument generates the correct MIC, but the EHR displays the wrong susceptibility category.

Repeating the MIC may not identify the problem.

The investigation should also consider:

- which breakpoint criteria were applied
- where the categorical interpretation was generated
- whether an expert rule modified the result
- whether organism or antimicrobial mappings were correct
- whether an interface transformed the result
- whether the LIS and EHR displayed the intended interpretation

This is an example of an **informatics discrepancy** rather than necessarily an analytical AST failure.

### AST Configuration as a Dependency Network

The same principle applies when the laboratory intentionally changes its AST configuration.

For example, implementing updated breakpoints may affect more than one location in the information pathway. Depending on the local architecture, the laboratory may need to evaluate:

- breakpoint configuration in the AST system
- expert rules and expected-phenotype logic
- selective or cascade-reporting rules
- organism and antimicrobial mappings
- middleware or LIS configuration
- comments or interpretive text
- downstream clinical decision support

A change can therefore be correct in one component while the overall workflow remains inconsistent.

> **Informatics principle:** When one component of an AST workflow changes, identify the systems and rules that depend on it, then verify the affected pathway end to end.

### AST and Clinical Decision Support

Decision support does not have to mean a pop-up alert.

In antimicrobial workflows, decision support may include:

- selective or cascade reporting
- interpretive comments
- reflex or protocol-based actions
- guidance presented with laboratory results
- targeted alerts when a result requires timely attention

The effectiveness of decision support depends not only on whether the underlying rule is technically correct, but also on **when, where, and to whom the information is presented**.

A technically accurate alert may provide little value if it appears too often, reaches the wrong user, interrupts the wrong part of the workflow, or does not lead to an actionable decision.

For this reason, clinical decision support should be treated as a **governed sociotechnical system**. Laboratories and clinical partners should define its intended purpose, monitor how it performs in practice, investigate unintended consequences, and modify or retire interventions that no longer provide the intended value.

> **Informatics principle:** Effective decision support delivers appropriate information to the appropriate person at an actionable point in the workflow.

## 5. Individual AST Results and Antibiograms

An individual AST report describes susceptibility information for a particular isolate.

An **antibiogram** summarizes susceptibility patterns across a defined collection of isolates.

<div className="science-illustration antibiogram-image" role="img" aria-label="Many isolate results aggregate into a cumulative antibiogram"><div className="science-copy"><small>Aggregation</small><strong>Many isolates become one population summary</strong><span>Inclusion rules, deduplication, organism identity, and antimicrobial mappings shape the cumulative percentage.</span></div><div className="aggregate-art"><div><i/><i/><i/><i/><i/><i/><i/><i/></div><em>→</em><span><b/><b/><b/><b/></span></div></div>

These serve different purposes.

Individual AST:

    Patient
       ↓
    Isolate
       ↓
    Organism-antimicrobial results
       ↓
    Clinical report

Antibiogram:

    Many qualifying isolates
             ↓
    Defined inclusion and analysis rules
             ↓
    Aggregated susceptibility data
             ↓
    Local susceptibility summary

Because antibiograms depend on aggregated laboratory data, their accuracy also depends on informatics.

Organism identification, antimicrobial results, duplicate-isolate handling, inclusion criteria, and data extraction must be represented and processed appropriately.

## 6. Governance Matters

AST knowledge does not remain static.

 <div className="micro-visual-copy"><small>Change governance</small><strong>An AST update is a controlled cycle</strong><span>Updated knowledge must be reviewed, configured, validated, deployed, and monitored across the systems that use it.</span></div>

Interpretive standards, laboratory policies, software, mappings, and expert-system configurations can change.

Laboratories therefore need defined processes for:

- reviewing relevant updates
- determining whether a change applies locally
- configuring laboratory systems
- validating changes before clinical use
- documenting implementation
- monitoring downstream reporting
- investigating unexpected results

Vendor defaults should not be assumed to remain appropriate indefinitely.

The laboratory retains responsibility for ensuring that the AST information it reports is appropriate for its validated workflow.

## The Microbiology Director's Role

A microbiology director does not need to personally program every expert rule or interface.

The director should understand enough of the AST information pathway to ask:

- Where was this interpretation generated?
- Which breakpoint or rule was applied?
- Is the organism-antimicrobial mapping correct?
- Did another system modify the result?
- Was the configuration validated?
- What changed since the last known correct result?

This is where microbiology expertise and informatics governance intersect.
