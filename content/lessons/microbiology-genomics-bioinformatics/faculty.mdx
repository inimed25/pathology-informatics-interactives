# Faculty Guide

## Teaching Focus

This lesson introduces microbial genomics as a **clinical informatics workflow**.

The goal is not to teach learners to become bioinformatics programmers. Learners should instead understand a sequencing pipeline well enough to evaluate:

- what biological or clinical question the pipeline is intended to answer
- what sequence data enter the computational workflow
- how those data are transformed
- what quality metrics support the result
- what references, databases, algorithms, and thresholds influence the output
- what conclusions the validated workflow can and cannot support
- how changes to computational components should be governed

Emphasize that **NGS describes a sequencing technology, not a single clinical application or bioinformatics workflow**.

A useful recurring question is:

> **What evidence does the sequence data actually support, and what additional assumptions or computational steps are required to reach the reported conclusion?**

---

## Key Teaching Points

### 1. Separate the Major Choices in a Sequencing Workflow

Learners commonly collapse several different concepts into “NGS.”

Help them distinguish:

**What is being sequenced?**
- whole microbial genome
- conserved microbial target
- selected genes or genomic regions
- nucleic acid from a complex specimen

**How are targets selected?**
- amplicon enrichment
- hybrid capture
- relatively untargeted approaches

**What type of reads are generated?**
- short reads
- long reads

**What happens computationally?**
- reference alignment
- de novo assembly
- hybrid assembly
- variant calling
- taxonomic classification
- gene or feature detection
- typing or relatedness analysis

These choices represent different dimensions of the workflow and should not be treated as interchangeable.

---

### 2. Teach Quality Metrics as Questions, Not Vocabulary

Rather than asking learners to memorize definitions, connect each metric to the question it answers.

- **Base quality:** Was this nucleotide likely read correctly?
- **Mapping quality:** Was this read likely placed at the correct reference location?
- **Depth:** How many reads support this genomic position?
- **Breadth:** How much of the intended target or genome was adequately interrogated?
- **Uniformity:** How evenly was coverage distributed?

A particularly useful misconception to challenge is:

> **High average depth does not guarantee adequate coverage of every clinically important region.**

If a learner says that a sequencing run is reliable because it has “high coverage,” ask what they mean by **coverage**.

---

### 3. Distinguish Detection From Genomic Context

Use the recurring *Klebsiella pneumoniae* example.

A pipeline may confidently establish:

> **A resistance gene is present.**

That conclusion does not automatically establish:

> **The resistance gene is located on a particular plasmid.**

Determining genomic context may require sequence information capable of resolving the surrounding region.

This creates a natural opportunity to discuss why long reads can be useful for resolving repetitive regions, plasmids, mobile genetic elements, structural features, and other genomic contexts that may be difficult to reconstruct using short reads alone.

Avoid teaching:

> **Long reads are better than short reads.**

Instead teach:

> **Read characteristics should match the analytical question.**

---

### 4. Keep Hybrid Capture and Hybrid Assembly Distinct

These terms sound similar but describe different parts of the workflow.

**Hybrid capture** is a laboratory target-enrichment strategy using complementary probes.

**Hybrid assembly** is a computational strategy that combines complementary sequencing information, commonly short and long reads.

This distinction is a useful check that learners understand the difference between:

**what material enters sequencing**

and

**what happens computationally after sequencing.**

---

### 5. Reference Alignment and De Novo Assembly Answer Different Questions

**Reference alignment** places reads relative to a selected reference sequence.

**De novo assembly** reconstructs longer sequences from overlapping reads without using a reference genome as the primary reconstruction framework.

Use the influenza example for reference-based analysis and the *K. pneumoniae* example for bacterial genome assembly.

Emphasize that reference choice itself is an analytical decision.

A reference genome provides both sequence information and a coordinate framework. Changing the reference can therefore affect alignment, variant representation, genomic coordinates, and downstream interpretation.

---

### 6. Assembly Metrics Require Context

Learners should recognize:

- a **contig** as a continuous reconstructed sequence
- **N50** as a measure of assembly contiguity

Do not encourage learners to interpret N50 as an overall score of genome quality.

A higher N50 does not independently establish that an assembly is more accurate or clinically appropriate.

Instead, assembly quality should be considered in relation to the intended use and other relevant characteristics.

---

### 7. Variant Calls and Consensus Sequences Are Computational Products

Variant calling involves determining whether sequence evidence supports a genomic difference.

Learners should recognize that this determination may depend on:

- depth
- base quality
- mapping quality
- read support
- local sequence context
- analytical thresholds

A **variant allele fraction (VAF)** describes the proportion of informative reads supporting a variant.

In microbial sequencing, an intermediate or low-frequency VAF may reflect several possibilities, including a mixed population, within-host diversity, contamination, artifact, or a biologically meaningful subpopulation.

The VAF alone does not establish which explanation is correct.

Similarly, a **consensus sequence** is a computational representation generated using defined rules. It should not automatically be interpreted as representing every microbial genome present in the specimen.

---

### 8. File Formats Represent Stages of Information

Learners do not need to memorize file specifications.

They should understand the conceptual progression:

**FASTQ**
→ reads and per-base quality information

**SAM/BAM**
→ alignment information

**VCF**
→ variant information

**FASTA**
→ sequence representation, such as a reference, contig, or consensus sequence

A useful simplified reference-based workflow is:

> **FASTQ → QC → alignment → SAM/BAM → variant calling → VCF**

An assembly-oriented workflow might instead include:

> **FASTQ → QC → assembly → contigs → FASTA**

These are conceptual examples rather than universal pipelines.

## Application-Specific Teaching

### Isolate Whole-Genome Sequencing

Use *K. pneumoniae* to ask progressively different questions:

1. Is a resistance determinant present?
2. What sequence surrounds it?
3. Is its genomic context resolved?
4. How similar is the isolate to other isolates?

The purpose is to show that increasingly specific biological claims require appropriate genomic evidence and analysis.

### Broad-Range Sequencing

Use a difficult bacterial identification based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

Ask:

> **If the best database matches include two closely related species, does the laboratory necessarily have enough information to report one species?**

Emphasize sequence quality, reference-database quality, taxonomic resolution, validated interpretation criteria, and reporting limitations.

### Metagenomic NGS

Use a CSF specimen containing a small amount of sequence assigned to a potential pathogen.

Ask learners whether:

> **sequence detected**

necessarily means:

> **organism truly present**

or:

> **organism caused the patient's disease**

It does not.

Controls, background signal, taxonomic specificity, abundance, specimen type, database quality, assay limitations, and clinical context may influence interpretation.

### Genomic Epidemiology

Present several highly similar *K. pneumoniae* isolates.

First distinguish common comparison frameworks:

- **SNP-based analysis** counts high-confidence nucleotide differences across genomic positions included in a defined analysis.
- **cgMLST** compares allele assignments across a standardized set of core-genome loci.
- **wgMLST** extends the gene-by-gene approach across a broader set of loci.

Emphasize that a SNP distance and an allele distance are different measurements and should not be treated as numerically interchangeable. Their interpretation also depends on the organism, reference or typing scheme, sequence quality, callable positions or loci, filtering, and the validated analytical framework.

Avoid teaching a universal SNP or allele cutoff for transmission. Thresholds are organism-, method-, and context-dependent.

Ask:

> **Does genomic similarity establish direct patient-to-patient transmission?**

No.

Genomic information can support a hypothesis of relatedness or shared ancestry, but it should be integrated with relevant epidemiologic evidence. Similar isolates may still reflect a shared source, unsampled intermediates, environmental reservoirs, or other transmission pathways, and sequence data alone generally do not establish transmission direction.

## Computational Provenance and Change Control

One of the most important informatics concepts in this lesson is that the same underlying sequence data can produce different downstream results when computational dependencies change.

Ask learners to imagine that the FASTQ files remain unchanged while the laboratory updates:

- a reference sequence
- a resistance database
- a taxonomic database
- pipeline software
- a parameter
- an analytical threshold

Then ask:

> **Could the reported result change?**

Potentially, yes.

This is why computational provenance may need to preserve information such as:

- reference accession and version
- database version
- software or pipeline version
- relevant parameters and thresholds
- QC results
- analytical configuration

Changes that could affect clinical results should undergo appropriate evaluation, documentation, and change control.

---

## Validation Discussion

Pipeline validation should evaluate the **intended clinical workflow**, not merely demonstrate that sequencing files can be generated.

Depending on intended use, relevant characteristics may include:

- limit of detection
- sensitivity
- specificity
- reproducibility
- relevant genomic regions or targets
- low-abundance or low-frequency findings
- mixed populations
- contamination
- expected failure modes
- computational thresholds
- final result generation

Do not turn this section into a general diagnostic-statistics lesson.

Instead emphasize:

> **Performance characteristics belong to a defined intended use.**

Sequencing performance may differ by organism, genomic region, sequence diversity, microbial burden, specimen background, coverage, or computational threshold.

Validation evidence should therefore support the actual claim the laboratory intends to report.

## Common Learner Misconceptions

Watch for these statements:

**“High depth means the whole genome was well covered.”**

→ Ask the learner to distinguish depth from breadth and uniformity.

**“A high-quality base must also be mapped correctly.”**

→ Distinguish base quality from mapping quality.

**“A higher N50 means the assembly is more accurate.”**

→ N50 describes contiguity, not overall correctness.

**“Long-read sequencing is better than short-read sequencing.”**

→ Return to intended use and the biological question.

**“Hybrid capture and hybrid assembly are basically the same thing.”**

→ Separate laboratory target enrichment from computational assembly.

**“If the resistance gene is detected, we know which plasmid carries it.”**

→ Separate presence from genomic context.

**“A consensus sequence represents everything in the specimen.”**

→ Discuss mixtures and minority populations.

**“If mNGS detects microbial reads, that organism caused the infection.”**

→ Separate sequence detection from biological presence and clinical significance.

**“If two isolates are genomically similar, one patient transmitted the organism to the other.”**

→ Integrate epidemiologic evidence.

**“Five SNPs and five cgMLST allele differences mean the same thing.”**

→ SNP-based and allele-based methods represent genomic differences differently; do not treat their distances as interchangeable.

**“There is one SNP or allele cutoff that proves transmission.”**

→ Interpretation depends on the organism, analytical method, reference or typing scheme, quality and filtering rules, and epidemiologic context.

**“If the FASTQ files did not change, the final result cannot change.”**

→ Discuss databases, references, algorithms, parameters, thresholds, and versioning.

## Suggested Teaching Scenario

A *Klebsiella pneumoniae* isolate undergoes WGS.

The pipeline detects a resistance determinant with strong sequence support.

Ask learners sequentially:

1. What can we conclude from detection alone?
2. What additional evidence would be needed to determine genomic context?
3. Would high average depth guarantee adequate interrogation of every relevant region?
4. How might short- versus long-read data affect the question?
5. What information should be preserved so the analysis can be reproduced?
6. What happens if the resistance database changes?
7. If another patient's isolate is highly genomically similar, what additional information is needed before making a transmission inference?

The goal is not to obtain one final answer.

The goal is to teach learners to **define the claim, identify the evidence supporting it, and recognize the computational assumptions between the two.**

---

## Discussion Prompts

- How would the informatics workflow differ among isolate WGS, broad-range 16S rRNA sequencing, and mNGS of a clinical specimen?
- Why might excellent average sequencing depth coexist with an inadequately characterized genomic region?
- When might long-read information change what a laboratory can conclude from a microbial genome?
- How could changing a reference or resistance database alter a result without changing the underlying reads?
- What is the difference between detecting a resistance gene and establishing its genomic context?
- If mNGS detects sequence assigned to a potential pathogen, what additional information should be considered before determining clinical significance?
- How do SNP-based relatedness analysis and cgMLST/wgMLST represent genomic differences differently?
- Why should SNP and allele distances not be treated as interchangeable or interpreted using a universal cutoff?
- Why does genomic similarity alone not prove direct transmission?
- Which components of a bioinformatics workflow should a microbiology director understand even if the director does not personally write the software?

---

## Faculty Emphasis

Learners do **not** need to leave this lesson able to:

- program a bioinformatics pipeline
- memorize command-line tools
- memorize specific aligners or variant callers
- calculate N50 by hand
- memorize platform-specific read lengths
- memorize universal coverage thresholds

They **should** leave able to follow sequence information through a computational workflow and recognize when a quality issue, analytical assumption, database change, or interpretive leap could alter a clinical conclusion.

---

## Teaching Principle

> **Follow the sequence data from generation to interpretation. At each step, ask what transformed the information, what evidence supports the resulting claim, what could change the result, and whether the interpretation remains within the validated intended use.**
